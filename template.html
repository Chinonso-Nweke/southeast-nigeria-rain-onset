Share of years (__FIRST__ to __LAST__) with a dry spell of the chosen length in the 30 days after each planting date. A dry day is a day under 1 mm of rain. This looks at rainfall only, from the rainfall source selected at the top. It ignores soil, crop and seed type, so it is not advice for a particular farm.

Best planting date

This combines two things: the dry spell risk you saw above and the cost of waiting. Planting later is safer from dry spells but loses part of the season. Say how costly waiting is, and the tool picks the date with the lowest total loss. It uses the dry spell length chosen above.

Because the rainfall sources disagree, it also finds a robust date: the one whose worst case across all sources and periods is smallest.

How it works: for each source and period (all years, and the last 20), loss = (share of years with a dry spell of the chosen length) + (cost of waiting per week x weeks after 1 March). The dry spell curve is averaged over seven dates so that one odd year cannot choose the date. The robust date minimises the worst regret: how much more loss it gives than the best date for whichever source and period turns out to be right. This is a simple model of rainfall risk and waiting cost only. It does not know your crop, soil or seed, so treat it as a way to see the trade-off, not as planting advice.

    Agreement is rated by a rule of thumb of my own, not an official standard: weak if yearly totals correlate below 0.5, or the later-start estimates differ by more than 10 days, or the change in yearly rain differs by more than 8 percentage points; strong only if all of these are much tighter. Neither source is a rain gauge measurement, so agreement does not prove either is right.

    What I learned

    Written in October 2026, about the results at that date. The numbers elsewhere on this page update each year, but this account does not.

    I started with one rainfall source (NASA POWER) and a written rule for when the rains start. It showed a later start at all five places, with a fall in yearly rain, and a statistical test said the shift in start date was unlikely to be luck.

    But a test of luck is not a test of whether the data is right. I compared it with a second source made independently (ERA5). It pointed the same way for the start date, but with much smaller shifts at four of the five places, and it showed only a small fall in yearly rain. Only at Onitsha did it agree on a large shift. The two sources also barely agreed on which years were wet or dry. Neither is a rain gauge measurement.

    So I stopped treating the later start as a finding. I built an auditor that rates how well the sources agree each time the page updates. I changed the best-date tool so it shows the spread across sources instead of one confident date. And I am preparing a farmer survey to gather independent evidence.

    What I would do next, with supervision: validate the sources against rain gauge records, use farmers' reports to calibrate which source to trust, and test whether the planting advice holds up under the remaining uncertainty.

    The rule used, its limits and the data checks

    Working rule for the start of the rains:

    • The rainfall figures are satellite and model estimates (NASA POWER on a grid of roughly 50 km, ERA5 of roughly 25 km), not rain gauge readings from a farm.
    • The rule is adapted from Sivakumar (1988): the first date with at least 20 mm in 3 days and no dry spell over 7 days in the next 30 days. I changed two things: I start on 1 March instead of 1 May, and I count a day under 1 mm as dry (the paper gives no value). It was made for drier zones, so it is a working choice here, not an official standard for south-east Nigeria.
    • A false start means an early wet spell was followed by a dry spell longer than 7 days. The percentage depends on the rule, so always read it together with the rule.
    • Past patterns do not guarantee future seasons. Nothing here is planting advice for a particular farm.
